## 2. Idea framing

- **Problem in user language:** “When I return to a client, I have to reconstruct the tabs and browser context needed to support them.”
- **Proposed solution:** A Chrome extension that restores a named, client-specific set of tabs and makes selected workspaces available to a support team.
- **Primary ICP:** Small outsourced customer-support agencies or support boutiques with roughly 5–20 browser-based agents, where agents repeatedly switch among recurring client accounts.
- **Likely buyer:** Support lead, operations lead, or agency owner—not necessarily the individual agent.
- **Non-ICP:** Individual knowledge workers, general-purpose tab hoarders, teams standardized on separate Chrome profiles, and enterprises whose managed-browser policies prohibit unapproved extensions.
- **Core hypothesis:** A team will pay to reduce repeated client-context setup when the workflow occurs daily and native browser organization is insufficient.
- **Important distinction:** Restoring tabs is a feature. Reducing client-switching time, mistakes, and onboarding inconsistency is the potentially payable outcome.

## 6. Commercial value

**Verdict: Mixed.**

The commercial outcome is reduced client-switching time and more consistent setup, not saved tabs by itself. Using the supplied estimates only, a daily loss of 10–20 minutes could become material across a team. For example, ten agents experiencing it every shift would imply substantial aggregate time loss, but such extrapolation is not evidence that this pattern actually holds across ten agents.

The most plausible payer is a support or agency lead accountable for utilization, service quality, or onboarding. A productivity or support-operations budget might fund the product if it demonstrably saves time or prevents mistakes. It is less compelling as an individual-agent subscription because free substitutes are abundant.

No replacement budget, purchasing authority, paid commitment, or retention signal has been established.

## 7. Independent-developer fit

**Verdict: Conditional.**

- **MVP scope:** A local prototype that saves and launches named client tab sets is manageable. Shared workspaces, authentication, synchronization, permissions, auditability, and admin controls would substantially expand the four-week scope.
- **Support burden:** Browser updates, lost workspace reports, conflicting edits, account recovery, and permission concerns could create disproportionate support work.
- **Distribution:** No channel is verified. Direct access to the twelve participants and three leads would be the best initial route if those contacts are available.
- **Deployment risk:** Managed organizations may prohibit extensions or require security review. Broad browsing permissions could make trust harder.
- **Regulation and liability:** The concept is not heavily regulated by default, but stored URLs, client names, internal tools, and authentication-related context may be sensitive.
- **Capital:** $500 is adequate for prototypes and direct validation, but not for broad paid acquisition or enterprise compliance.
- **Founder skills:** Unknown. The fit assumes you can build and maintain a Chrome extension plus a minimal synchronization service.

The product remains indie-shaped only if the first version avoids enterprise administration and proves demand before building real-time team infrastructure.

## 8. Short-term return: 30–90 days

**Target:** $500 MRR within 90 days.

**Odds: Low.**

The best plausible route is team-level founder-led sales: secure several small agencies on minimum team plans instead of acquiring dozens of individual users. Even then, reaching $500 MRR likely means about ten $50 accounts or an equivalent mix of 42–72 seats at the unverified adjacent price range.

The odds are low because:

- There is no verified payment intent.
- The acquisition channel is unknown.
- Native features and bookmarks may make the improvement too incremental.
- Admin restrictions could disqualify otherwise interested teams.
- Team synchronization—the requested differentiator—is also the costlier portion to build and support.

The route is falsified if accessible leads will not authorize even a conditional paid pilot, if agents do not reuse the workspace during observed shifts, or if managed-browser policy prevents installation in the intended teams.

A more defensible 30-day success threshold is **one paid team pilot**, not $500 MRR. The 90-day target should remain conditional on obtaining that first commitment before the main build.

## 9. Entry direction

### Primary wedge: repeatable client launchpads for small outsourced support teams

- **Who:** Support agencies with 5–20 browser-based agents who rotate among recurring client accounts.
- **Job to be done:** “When an agent starts or returns to a client assignment, open the approved working context immediately and consistently.”
- **MVP shape:** Named client workspace templates; one-click launch; simple update/ownership control; basic sharing through an intentionally lightweight mechanism. Do not begin with live collaboration, complex roles, analytics, or enterprise administration.
- **Buyer promise:** Faster client switching and consistent client setup across agents.
- **First-user source:** The reported participants and support leads, if contactable. External communities remain unverified.
- **Fourteen-day metric:** At least three agents across two teams use a client launchpad on three separate shifts, and at least one lead accepts a paid-pilot offer.
- **Commercial packaging to test:** A small-team minimum rather than a purely individual plan. The price must be tested, not inferred from the adjacent tool.

### Backup wedges

None recommended until the primary wedge is tested. Expanding into generic personal tab management would move directly toward strong free substitutes and dilute the buyer.

### Explicitly do not start with

- A generic “restore all my tabs” extension.
- Enterprise-wide deployment, SSO, audit logs, or complex administration.
- Support for multiple browsers.
- AI-generated workspaces.
- Real-time collaborative editing.
- A four-week polished build before obtaining a paid-pilot commitment.

## 11. Next 48 hours

1. **Re-interview five of the twelve participants, including at least two of the daily users.** Ask them to reconstruct the last specific occurrence, show how they solved it, identify the exact tabs and state involved, and explain why saved groups, profiles, or bookmarks were insufficient. Pass if at least three describe a recent repeated workflow without prompting and permit observation of the next occurrence; fail if the pain remains hypothetical or native features solve it.

2. **Run a concierge test with three agents across at least two teams.** Manually create one client launchpad per agent using a clickable mock or minimal local prototype, then observe use across two shifts. Pass if at least two agents independently reuse it and each demonstrates at least five minutes saved per shift; fail if reuse requires reminders, restoration needs application state beyond URLs, or installation is blocked.

3. **Make a concrete paid-pilot offer to the three support leads.** Offer the narrowly defined team launchpad to a small cohort with a start date, team minimum, and refundable first month; ask for payment or written procurement approval—not general interest. Pass if at least one lead commits money or begins an identifiable approval process; fail if all defer without a specific buying step.
